## PART 1: THE PUZZLE

### (\~1,500–1,800 words)

| Step | Argument Move | Concept Introduction | Literature |
|----|----|----|----|
| 1.1 | Democratic representation has a constitutive communicative dimension: it requires address between representative and represented, not merely alignment of preferences or policy output. (Modest framing — this dimension is widely recognized; the strong claim that representation *is* communication is not needed and should not be asserted.) | **communication as constitutive dimension**; **publics = social groups**, inclusive of non-citizens, as the starting ontology of who might be represented | Alcoff 2020 (epigraph/framing); Young 2000/2002; Disch 2011 (political learning enmeshed in a struggle for power) |
| 1.2 | SMP promises to organize this communication institutionally: single-member districts supply an identifiable addressee; candidate-centered elections create incentives for attentiveness; under favorable conditions, recursive communication obtains. | **SMP's communicative claim**; **promissory, anticipatory, recursive, gyroscopic, surrogate** (Mansbridge's vocabulary, introduced together) | Mansbridge 2003; Mansbridge 2018 (recursive communication) |
| 1.3 | But electoral design does not distribute communicative attention equally. Strategic value varies: constituents ≠ eligible ≠ likely ≠ decisive. Representatives communicate selectively, and the selectivity is predictable. | **strategic value** (first pass); **selective address**; line A advances: publics → districts → constituents → *some* constituents | Pottle 2025 (strategic value); Mansbridge 2003 (surrogate/gyroscopic show communication isn't only dyadic-district) |
| 1.4 | The problem deepens when strategic devaluation is durable rather than rotational — when it overlaps with social power. Some groups are permanent minorities: not outnumbered this cycle but structurally positioned such that their strategic value does not rise. | **social power** (Fricker's sense, introduced as breadcrumb); **permanent minority** (structural, not numerical); **durable vs. rotational** | Fricker 2007; Young 2000 (structural inequality); Horowitz + [locate second cite] on the limits of electoral engineering — this is the pivot |
| 1.5 | **Puzzle statement.** These observations give reason to question SMP's communicative promise. But existing accounts stop at unequal inclusion. Three things remain untheorized: (a) whether institutions beyond the electoral shape representative communication; (b) what happens to those bracketed as strategically irrelevant — whether they exit the representational field or remain within it, positioned differently; (c) what changes when a group receives not too little of the state's first face but too much of its second. This paper argues they do not disappear; they are repositioned as *objects* of representative communication. | **first face / second face** (breadcrumb, cited only); **object** (named as destination); **represented-about vs. represented-to** (the paper's operative vocabulary, introduced here) | Soss & Weaver 2016/2017 (first mention); [paper's own claim] |

**Breadcrumbs planted:** social power (1.4 → Part 2 → Part 3); second face (1.5 → 3.2 → Part 4); object / represented-about (1.5 → 3.3 → Part 4); permanent minority (1.4 → 2.2 → 4.1).

**Drafting note:** keep the "represented-about / represented-to" pair as the working vocabulary throughout. Avoid "non-subject" and other deficit constructions — they strain against the too-much-presence finding the paper relies on.

## PART 2: THE BRACKETING SEQUENCE

### (\~2,000 words)

### Structure: each step takes the prior seriously and removes one bracket. Nobody is wrong.

| Step | Argument Move | Concept Introduction | Literature |
|----|----|----|----|
| 2.1 | **The equality assumption.** SMP's communicative virtues are real but conditional. Mansbridge's defense of candidate-centered single-member representation as producing stronger incentives to listen and speak than closed-list PR proceeds under a bracketed assumption of rough equality among constituents. Name the bracket explicitly; the argument that follows is a sequence of removals. | **the equality assumption** (a condition, not a finding) | Mansbridge 2018 — **cite the bracketing passage precisely; the whole sequence rests on it being a real bracket rather than a summary simplification**; Pottle 2025 fn. 13 as evidence the assumption travels forward unexamined |
| 2.2 | **Removing the bracket within the electoral frame.** Electoral incentives push office-seekers to orient rhetoric toward strategically valuable constituencies. Their testimony is treated *as if* more credible and incorporated into public-facing speech; mainstream elite discourse comes to be composed of understandings drawn from the advantaged standpoint; media uptake spills the bias into broader circuits of public knowledge-making. Two harms follow: hermeneutic injustice in the resources circulated, testimonial injustice in how citizens relate to one another as knowers. | **the hermeneutic economy of electoral politics**; **practical testimonial inferiority**; line E advances: strategic value now has epistemic consequence | Pottle 2025 (anchor); Fricker 2007 (vocabulary); Medina (footnote only) |
| 2.3 | **The closest approach, and where it stops.** Pottle comes nearest this paper's concern in noting that dog-whistling rhetoric about the immorality and irresponsibility of poor Black communities casts those citizens as unreliable narrators of their own experience. Speech *about* a group, producing harm. But the harm is theorized as spillover: elite rhetoric feeds prejudice, corroding citizens' horizontal relations as testimony-givers. Being spoken-about damages a group's standing; it is not yet theorized as a *position within* the representative relationship. | **spillover vs. position** — the distinguishing move, named here | Pottle 2025 (engage the passage directly, in his terms); Shelby 2016 |
| 2.4 | **The hinge: two boundaries.** (a) *The extension by analogy.* Pottle extends the argument to perpetual nonvoters and noncitizens on the grounds that they too occupy a position of strategic irrelevance. The assimilation is where the account stops short: strategic irrelevance and negative strategic value are not degrees of the same thing. The irrelevant are passed over; the negatively valued are used. (b) *Institutions of authorization.* His reform horizon is broad — RCV, campaign finance, sortition and nonelectoral alternatives all considered seriously, sortition rejected on grounds about bidirectional communication this paper endorses. But every institution considered is an institution of *authorization*. None of the coercive or administrative institutions that shape who is present in a district, who is eligible, who is credible, and what interpretive resources exist about a population appears as a site of representative communication. **The question:** what follows if the second face is not adjacent to representative communication but constitutive of some groups' position within it? | **negative strategic value ≠ strategic irrelevance** (named; theorized in 3.4); **institutions of authorization** (the boundary line) | Pottle 2025 (fn. on nonvoters/noncitizens — cite directly; reform section); [transition to Part 3] |

**Footnote at 2.4** (one sentence, and where the Saward observation goes): the constructivist vocabulary Pottle works within (Disch, Mansbridge, Urbinati, Young) theorizes the represented as unequal participants in inquiry; the claim-making tradition (Saward, Rai, Castiglione & Pollak) theorizes representation as constructed performance. The two have not been brought together on the question of what happens to a group that appears in representative discourse as *content*.

**Cut from v1:** legislature-as-hermeneutical-institution. The MA committee anecdote moves to 4.2 and is relabeled — it is agenda control plus anticipated reputational cost, not hermeneutical injustice.

## PART 3: THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

### (\~3,000–3,300 words)

| Step | Argument Move | Concept Introduction | Literature |
|----|----|----|----|
| 3.1 | **Forms of representative communication expand.** Part 2 assumed representative communication is speech and responsiveness. It is broader: symbols, rhetoric, agenda-setting, priorities, and policy design. Policy designs communicate to and about target populations, sending messages about worth, legitimacy, and standing in the polity. This is representative communication in the constructive sense. | **policy design as communication**; **social construction of target populations**; **performance as representation**; line B advances: speech → symbol → policy design | Schneider & Ingram 2019; Saward 2010/2014/2018; Rai 2021; Disch (in body, not table — she is the bridge between registers); Castiglione & Pollak |
| 3.2 | **Representative communication is multi-institutional.** If policy design is communicative, then any institution that designs and implements policy is a site of representative communication. The state's second face — coercive, administrative, supervisory — makes claims about who groups are, what they deserve, how they stand to the polity. **On the claim-maker:** these claims are made by identifiable actors — legislators, prosecutors, governors, police executives — and become institutionally durable through policy design and feedback. The institution is the medium of durability, not itself the agent. | **second face as communicative institution**; **multi-institutional representative communication**; **claims have makers, audiences, and content** | Soss & Weaver 2016/2017 (theoretical, not yet case); Saward 2010/2014; Lerman & Weaver 2014 (state-building) |
| — | *Footnote at 3.2:* a stronger distributed-claim reading is available, on which institutions make claims without identifiable claimants. Nothing in the argument turns on it. |  |  |
| 3.3 | **The subject/object distinction.** Subject-position requires not merely presence in representative discourse but (a) being addressed rather than spoken-about, (b) having one's testimony received with appropriate credibility, (c) access to hermeneutical resources adequate to one's experience, (d) being constructed as *audience* of representative claims rather than only their *content*. Object-position is the structured absence of these conditions: not removal from representation but a specific place within it. Positions are not mutually exclusive — a group may hold subject-position with a surrogate representative while occupying object-position in dominant discourse. | **subject/object of representative communication**; **audience vs. content** (extending Saward: claims have objects too); **simultaneity** (positions co-held — this is what prevents the framework from covering everyone who is not a swing voter) | Young 2000/2002; Fricker 2007; Medina; Saward 2010; Mansbridge 2003 (surrogate as partial subject-position across district lines) |
| 3.4 | **Two routes into object-position — and the mechanism that distinguishes them.** Groups of low strategic value *may* be objectified residually: to the degree they appear in elite rhetoric, they do so incidentally, as illustration, background, the population a policy is about. Groups of negative strategic value are *likely* to be objectified functionally, because the construction is useful for the representative's relationship with another audience. The distinction is mechanistic: **electoral incentives produce less value; policy design and second-face communication produce negative value.** Object-position acquires durability through policy feedback — punitive design communicates deviance, which feeds back into further devaluation. Path dependence is the account of how this sediments, not a separate argument. | **residual vs. functional objectification**; **negative strategic value** (theorized here, demonstrated in Part 4); **institutional production** (not failure to include but active making); **sedimentation** | Schneider & Ingram 2019 (deviants; feedback); Pottle 2025 (contrasted — irrelevance vs. negative value); Acharya et al. 2018; King & Smith 2014 |

**Internal order:** B (form) → C (site) → A+D (who is positioned, theorized) → E (mechanism). Part 4 follows the same sequence.

**Drafting notes:** - 3.3(b) and (c) are Fricker restated and 3.3(a) is close to Young. That is fine and correct as progressive building — but the definitional weight of the contribution sits in 3.4, not 3.3. Write 3.3 economically. - Do not overclaim against Pottle in 3.4. He identifies racial distancing and traces its testimonial consequences; he does not theorize it as a representational position. That is sufficient.

## PART 4: THE CASE (RCS Communities / Carceral Constituencies)

### (\~2,500–2,800 words)

### Evidentiary role: demonstration, not extension. State this in the introduction.

| Step | Argument Move | Concept Introduction | Literature |
|----|----|----|----|
| 4.1 | **Publics/groups.** Begin where the paper began. RCS communities are social groups positioned by intersecting structures of race and class — and partly constituted by the state's institutional address. "Criminal," "felon," "offender" are state-produced categories that become social identities. | **state-produced group categories** (extends Young/Alcoff: some groups are made through state address) | Young 2000; Alcoff 2020; Soss & Weaver 2016/2017 (race-making); Lerman & Weaver 2014 |
| 4.2 | **Districts and constituents — the second face intervenes at every stage.** Incarceration removes people from districts; prison gerrymandering counts bodies without political presence; felony disenfranchisement removes formal constituent status; custodial citizenship produces persons within the district who are governed but not addressed. The narrowing the literature attributes to electoral design is, for this population, driven by coercive institutions. **MA anecdote here:** a committee chair kills a bill restoring voting rights to incarcerated people without granting it a hearing, in probable violation of the state constitution; colleagues decline to object and later hesitate to cosponsor. This is agenda control plus anticipated reputational cost — negative strategic value foreclosing the first face procedurally, with no audience to make the foreclosure costly. | **custodial citizenship**; **prison gerrymandering**; line A applied — and the second face shown intervening at three points, not one | Lerman & Weaver 2014; Weaver et al. 2019; Soss & Weaver 2017; [MA anecdote — brief, in text or substantial footnote] |
| 4.3 | **Negative strategic value, demonstrated.** These communities are not merely less valuable; association is a liability and distancing signals something to other audiences. The "tough on crime" representative claim is *addressed to* one public and *about* another. They are the content of the claim, not its audience — the functional objectification theorized at 3.4. Note the leverage: Pottle observes that much elite rhetoric is empty posturing disconnected from governing intentions. That concession is what makes the communicative harm irreducible to policy effect, and it is equally available here with a target in place of an audience. | Functional objectification demonstrated; **instrumentalization** | Schneider & Ingram 2019; Soss & Weaver 2016/2017; Pottle 2025 (posturing/Mayhew — used constructively) |
| 4.4 | **Communicative construction as object.** The full framework applied: policy design (mandatory minimums, three-strikes, broken-windows as messages about dangerousness and lesser citizenship); performance (policing as visible spectacle of state authority on racialized bodies); rhetoric (discourse constructing "criminal" as identity); and the cumulative feedback that sediments these constructions across courts, police, prisons, legislatures, executives. | Form, site, position, sedimentation — all four lines converging | Soss & Weaver 2016/2017; Weaver et al. 2019; Acharya et al. 2018; King & Smith 2014 |
| 4.5 | **Subject-position denied — and contested.** Simultaneously constructed as objects and excluded from subject-position: hermeneutical marginalization; testimony received through the lens of the constructions the carceral state produced; institutional removal from sites of address. **Guard against the agency problem explicitly:** object-position describes a position within institutional discourse, not a description of these communities, who are political agents contesting that position. Weaver, Prowse and Piston's finding — too much knowledge, too little power — is the evidence, not decoration. Note here that object-position fits the too-much-presence finding better than "exclusion" does; this is why the paper's vocabulary avoids deficit terms. | Two faces reinforcing; **object-position as excess presence, not absence** | Young 2002; Fricker 2007; Medina; Weaver et al. 2019; Lerman & Weaver 2014 |
